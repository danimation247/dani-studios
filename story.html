The Making of an Animator

A totally professional account of how a kid with a sketchbook became an animator. Mostly.

Beginnings

Long before animation was a career plan, it was just a hobby. In Grade 6, our artist started drawing by copying the characters on book covers. Ben 10, PAW Patrol, Kim Possible, if it was on a cover, it was getting drawn. What started as casual practice quickly turned into a serious habit.

Discovering Manga

Then a classmate asked him to start drawing manga. He had no idea what manga was, but he wasn't about to admit that. He went home, searched for it on Google, and found out there were tutorial apps that could teach him.

There was just one problem: he didn't own a phone. So he did what every kid does and used his mom's. The catch was that he could only get to her phone in the evenings, so his plan was simple. Wait until everyone was asleep, then quietly wake up in the middle of the night to draw.

Was he dedicated? Not really. He just wanted to impress his friend. And somehow, everyone loved his art. He can confidently say he had zero competition back then. He was exceptional.

The Eraser Situation

Things were going smoothly until the night he gathered his materials and realized his eraser was missing. He searched everywhere and never found it. So he made a decision: no room for mistakes.

That's when he learned to improvise. He worked around the small mistakes instead of erasing them, and after a while, he got comfortable enough to move on to drawing with a pen. Looking back, he says that's what really helped him grow.

Anime Era

The manga his friend recommended changed his style. He started drawing anime characters, and that became his new favorite thing to draw.

Craig of the Creek and the Flipbook Phase

In his last year of middle school, he discovered Craig of the Creek. That show got him thinking about animation beyond drawing on paper. He also fell in love with the idea of voice acting, which he would dig into later. (That's a story for another day.)

Around the same time, he found Andymations, a YouTuber who made flip books. That was his first real taste of animation. His early work was all stick figures, but he found the process so tedious that he went looking for an easier way. That search led him to FlipaClip.

By the time he finished middle school, he was drawing on paper, taking photos of the frames, and importing them into FlipaClip. Nothing fancy, just stick people. Still, he learned a big lesson: even a one-minute clip takes a serious amount of time. He almost gave up, but he loved animation too much to stop looking for a way to make it work.

The Stop Motion Detour

Next came stop motion. He experimented with toy cars and thought it looked like the most satisfying way to make animation. It was tedious, but it was also fun. Still, he stepped away from animation and went back to digital art. For some reason, he kept using FlipaClip, even for his art.

The Boondocks Glow-Up

In senior high school, a friend introduced him to The Boondocks. He loved the show's perspective and its art style, and it started to change how he drew. He stopped only drawing white anime characters. Now he mostly draws characters who look like the ones he admires, and he proudly says he's "for the cause" now.

Words on Paper

Back in senior high school, he wrote small poems and story notes. Nothing serious, just for the fun of it. One of them, a short poem called Wooden Box, got a few reads, and his friends loved it.

That reaction sparked something in one friend, whom he likes to think of as his motivation. The friend decided to write one too, and that's where a writing path was forged. The two of them started writing short stories just to see whose was best. The friend usually came out on top. As far as he knows, that friend has since given up on writing, though he's not entirely sure.

Randy Cunningham and the Rigging Rabbit Hole

During his complition of high school, he discovered Randy Cunningham: 9th Grade Ninja. The show taught him that a character doesn't need tons of detail to be memorable, especially when you're a one-man studio. It also rekindled his love for animation. While digging into it, he found out the show wasn't entirely hand-drawn frame by frame. It was party rigged.

That started his journey into 2D rigging. He wanted to learn Toon Boom Harmony, the industry standard, but he was too broke to afford it. So he tried Moho instead. It took some getting used to, but now he can build characters in it.

The irony? He still can't rig his characters. He watched tons of tutorials and still hit a wall. So he took a hiatus.

Anime, Blender, and Scenes

Here's another thing: he watches a lot of anime. That's where he noticed that anime often mixes 2D characters with 3D backgrounds. That inspired another side quest, this time into Blender, a free 3D program.

By then, he had finished high school and started his first year of university. Today, he has mastered the basics of Blender and is creating his own scenes.

University, Instagram, and a Break

When he started university, he enrolled in IT. Art still came first, though. He wanted to build a stronger digital portfolio, so he spent his time making digital art to post on social media, mainly Instagram and Pinterest under @danimation247.

The plan didn't work out the way he hoped. He couldn't keep up with the algorithm, and his work wasn't reaching people. So he took a break from posting.

Ping Meister: Starting Small

Then a wave of inspiration hit. Why not make a game? It would be something different, and it would be a way to create without the pressure of an audience. He wanted to start small so the project wouldn't overwhelm him, and that's how Ping Meister was born. It was his first game.

A Web Novel, Just Because

His next project was a web novel game. He didn't have a special reason for it. He just wanted to try it.

"Just cuz I can."

That phrase has become his motto. It reminds him that he doesn't need permission to try something new, and it keeps him going into the next project.

Where Things Stand

From a kid sneaking sketches at night to a university student building 3D scenes, the journey has been messy, funny, and full of detours. And the story isn't over yet.
